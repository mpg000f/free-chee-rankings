Best and Worst Draft Values

The committee also wanted a better answer to the auction debate than “that guy cost too much,” so here is how the number is built. We took all 894 quarterbacks, running backs, receivers and tight ends drafted in this league from 2022 through 2025 and recorded what each one cost and what he scored across that entire season, then scaled the season down to a seven-game share so it lines up with where we are now. Using the whole year rather than just its first seven weeks keeps a hot September from being mistaken for a good investment, and it stops a slow starter from being written off. We then fit a curve through that history, separately for each position, using the median rather than the average so one freak campaign cannot set the bar for everybody else.

Hand that curve a price and it tells you what a typical player at that price has been worth over seven games. A $20 quarterback comes back at 95.0 points. A $20 running back, 71.4. That is the Expected column, and Value is simply what your guy actually did minus what the curve said he would.

Every pick counts here, all 229 of them, whether the player is still on your roster or not. You bought him in August and he is yours forever. Trading a corpse does not make it disappear and cutting a bust does not refund the money, so the ledger follows the man who spent it.

Separating the positions matters. A $30 quarterback and a $30 receiver don’t carry the same historical scoring expectation in this league. The QB curve rises quickly at the cheaper prices and then flattens, so every extra dollar does not buy the same amount of expected production. The TE curve is much flatter at the expensive end. That is a reason to be careful paying up, not proof that an expensive tight end has never worked. These are seven-week results, and injuries and bye timing are still part of the raw scores.

Six of the twelve worst picks in the league are quarterbacks, a combined 456.8 points underwater, which is comfortably more than a full season of a good one set on fire. The bottom three — Tua, Doza and Dart — have produced 30.0 points between them for $90. That is ninety dollars spent on three men who have mostly been standing near football.

Tua is the worst pick in the league and he is not even on the roster that drafted him any more, which is the one mercy available to Nick Wright. Shipping him to Free Paid stopped the bleeding on the field. It did not get the twenty dollars back, and it does not get him off this list.

One more look at where the money went. Of the $3,153 spent at auction, $1,373 of it went to quarterbacks, and the position sits 315.5 points below expectation. Running backs are 340.5 points above it on $871. Tight ends are 250.9 above on a laughable $121. Receivers are 24.9 underwater on $788.

Every person in this room spent August terrified of missing a quarterback in superflex, bid each other into the stratosphere, and the value was at running back and tight end the whole time — sitting there for three dollars with Chuba Hubbard’s and Isaiah Likely’s names on it.

Dollars Lost to Injury

Now for everyone’s favorite defense: “My team would be good if it were healthy.” The committee put a price on that claim. We multiplied each drafted player’s auction cost by the share of his team’s games he missed to injury. A $40 player who missed two of seven games counts as $11.43 lost. Byes don’t count. A player who left early, played less than a quarter of his team’s snaps and carried an injury designation counts as half a missed game. The cost stays with whoever drafted him, even if he has since been traded or dropped.

The distinction between injured and unavailable matters. Josh Jacobs’ six weeks on the exempt list do not count as a football injury. Neither does a healthy backup sitting there while the starter plays. You can make a bad pick, suffer an injury or do both. The committee is trying to keep the paperwork straight.

The league has spent $378.56 of its $3,153 on men who were not available. That is twelve cents on every dollar, across 217.5 player-games and something like 1,597 points of production that simply never happened.

Three teams have a real argument about who has suffered most, and they have each won a different category. Stewed has lost the most money at $46.56. Dickens has lost the most weeks at 25 player-games. Ger has lost the largest share of his budget at 23.0%, and his list — Caleb Williams, Breece Hall, Jordan Mason, DJ Moore, Stefon Diggs — is not a set of injuries, it is a waiting room.

Art has lost the most actual football of anybody: 29 player-games and 197.2 points of expected production. But almost all of it came from cheap players, so the bill stops at $36.40. Losing a dollar flier nine times wrecks a lineup without ever touching the wallet.

Pelosi deserves an award for efficiency. He has wasted $35.71 and it is all one man. Four of his five real investments hit; the fifth is Jaxson Dart, who went on injured reserve in Week 3 and has been quietly billing him ever since.

And then there is Work Ass at $5.31, 2.7% of his budget and the lowest figure in the league. The healthiest roster in the league by a mile, and still in the basement. There is no injury defense available to him, no bad luck to point at, no appeal to file. Work Ass drafted a bad team and then watched it stay in perfect health while being bad.
